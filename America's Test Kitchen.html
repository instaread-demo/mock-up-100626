What You Need to Know

A good pizza stone is easy to use and helps to transform a home oven into one that can produce pizzas as delicious as those from your local slice shop. Our winning baking steels, the Original Baking Steel and Nerd Chef Steel Stone, Standard ¼", turned out professional-level pizzas with perfectly charred exteriors and tender interiors. They’re a little heavy and awkward to lift, but once in the oven, they’re a breeze to use and will last forever. The Cook N Home Pizza Grilling Stone Set is our Best Buy. It comes in a set of six square tiles that fit together to make one large baking surface. Each tile weighs less than a pound and a half, so they’re easy to move in and out of the oven as needed. Compared to most other pizza stones, it’s much more maneuverable.

The blazing-hot oven in a real pizzeria can produce pizza with flavorful, crisp, deeply browned crust; melty, bubbling cheese; and hot, savory-sweet tomato sauce all in a matter of minutes. At home, though, pizza rarely reaches those heights. While a professional oven can hit temperatures upwards of 800 degrees, home ovens typically top out at 500 to 550 degrees. Heating a baking stone or steel in a hot oven for an hour before baking turns out pizzas that come closest to those produced in restaurant ovens. We baked pie after pie to see which ones perform best and are the easiest to use.

Why We Prefer Baking Steels Over Ceramic Baking Stones

All the stones and steels in our lineup produced very good pizzas, but the pizzas baked on steels had crusts that were a bit more airy and moist and perceptibly more tender. The stone-baked crusts, on the other hand, were slightly drier, chewier, and even a smidge tougher than ideal. In fact, the steels topped our rankings, coming out ahead of every stone option. Here’s why we think steels are the way to go.

  • Heat Capacity and Conduction: Steels tend to produce lighter, more tender pizzas. This is because steel and ceramic transfer heat at different rates: Steel is more dense than porous ceramic, which means it holds more heat per cubic inch. Steel is also a better heat conductor than ceramic, so baking steels “throw” their heat into dough much faster, causing pizzas to rise higher and brown more quickly than ceramic baking stones do. They also finish cooking a bit more quickly. Our temperature tests revealed that while all the models measured 500 degrees after an hour of preheating, the steels’ temperatures dropped faster after receiving the dough than those of the stone models. That might sound bad, but it means that they quickly gave away more of their heat—sending it into the pizza, where it counts. Ceramic stones transfer heat more slowly than steels. As a result, stones worked a bit more steadily and gradually to brown the crust—but this also meant that the crust lost slightly more moisture as the pizza baked. The crusts produced on the steels were more on par with those produced in a professional pizzeria: supermoist, with a tender, chewy interior and a crisp, charred exterior.
  • Durability and Maintenance: Baking steels are made of carbon steel and baking stones are typically made from cordierite ceramic. Like skillets made from the same material, baking steels will last a lifetime, especially if treated with a little care. Baking stones, while also capable of lasting for years and years, are susceptible to cracking if exposed to moisture or if dropped. They require a bit more attention to keep in good shape.

Other Considerations

  • Weight and Handling: Baking stones and steels can be heavy—the heaviest in our lineup weighed a whopping 27 pounds. A heavier stone or steel is more effective for cooking because it increases thermal mass in the oven, but it’s difficult to move around. Once you get it into the oven, it’s not a bad idea to leave it in there (read our FAQ below to learn more), even when you’re cooking other foods. If maneuvering a heavy stone or steel is a concern for you, we tested and recommend lightweight tiles that can be arranged together on an oven rack to form a large baking surface.

The Tests

  • Bake Thin-Crust Pizza on each model, monitoring the temperature throughout the process, starting with an hour of preheating plus baking the pizzas
  • Bake simple crusty bread on each model, noting the brownness, height, and crispness of the crust
  • Evaluate the weight, shape, and handling of each model
  • Assess the condition of the models at the conclusion of testing

  • Easy to clean and maintain

FAQs

To store baking stones or steels, you can avoid a lot of hoisting and heaving by simply leaving them in the oven on a rack most of the time, where they act as what engineers call “thermal ballast,” helping to keep the oven temperature steady. Just be aware that if you do this, they may add a few minutes to preheating times. When using the oven, don’t place pans directly on the steel or stone unless you want extra heat (and browning) applied to the bottom of your food, as we do with our recipe for Easier Roast Turkey and Gravy. And if you’re baking delicate items such as cookies or cakes, it’s best to take out the steel or stone to avoid disrupting the heat-circulation pattern of your oven and baking the food unevenly. (It’s the same reason our recipes often suggest baking cookies one sheet at a time.) Finally, don’t be tempted to place your stone or steel on the oven floor; many ovens have vents there that for safety reasons must not be blocked.

In general, you don’t have to wash a baking steel or stone; you can merely scrape off any food blobs or excess char from baking pizzas and call it a day. Any stains that remain will be rendered into harmless ash the next time the stone or steel is preheated to 500 degrees. You shouldn’t use soap on an unglazed ceramic stone, as it can get trapped in the stone’s pores and emerge to make a soapy-tasting pizza. If you wet the stone, allow it to thoroughly air-dry before heating it, as the moisture can expand during heating and crack the stone. If you really want to scrub your steel, treat it as you would a carbon-steel or cast-iron skillet: with hot water, no soap, and a stiff brush, followed by thorough drying and a light application of oil to prevent rust. If rust does form, scrub it off and then oil and heat the steel to reseason it.

Since a baking steel heats the bottom of a pizza crust faster than a ceramic stone does, the top of a pizza baked on it can sometimes emerge undercooked in some ovens. If you find that your pizza is cooking unevenly, you might try one of the methods our test cooks have come up with to compensate for the faster heat transfer of steel. First, switch the oven to “broil” as soon as the pizza goes into the oven. This directs more intense heat to the top of the pizza, so the cheese and toppings are ready at the same time as the bottom crust. If this technique doesn’t help, move the steel to a lower rack nearer the center of the oven after switching to “broil” and set the oven to 450 degrees instead of 500 to slow down the cooking a bit and to direct heat at the top of the pizza. If you bake a lot of bread, you’ll also have to keep the steel’s faster heat transfer in mind. That might mean turning down the oven temperature called for in your recipe to keep the bottom of the loaf from overbrowning before the top is done, or moving the steel to a different rack. There’s a bit of trial and error involved in using a baking steel, but we think the long-term results are worth the effort.
