Saving What Is Left of Agency Adjudication

Paul R. Verkuil

Twitter LinkedIn Print

It is much too soon to give up on good-cause removal protections for administrative law judges.

Tweet Share Post Email Print Link

Richard J. Pierce, Jr., argued in a recent essay in The Regulatory Review that “it may be time to give up on agency adjudication.” I believe that Pierce, whom I greatly admire, has surrendered too soon. The U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Slaughter, which denied “for cause” removal protections to independent agency commissioners, reserved the question of its applicability to adjudicative officials such as administrative law judges (ALJs) for good reasons. First, ALJs are inferior, not principal officers. Second, they do not make “executive” or policy decisions; they adjudicate. Third, depriving them of “for cause” protections implicates the Due Process Clause. Finally, doing so would open a very large can of worms. Let me explain.

As Pierce notes, much of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) is devoted to ensuring that ALJs are given independence from agency interference with their decision-making. Removal restrictions are at the heart of the statute, long thought of as the “administrative constitution.” Surely, declaring part of that statute unconstitutional would be an extraordinary repudiation of foundational law. The APA was a victory that conservatives back in 1946 finally achieved against the perceived excesses of the New Deal. Why would this generation of judicial conservatives want to give it back? Moreover, what would they gain?

Most of the federal government’s nearly 2,000 ALJs adjudicate Social Security disability cases. Depriving ALJs of removal protections would bring forth a wave of procedural due process cases. Although the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Goldberg v. Kelly, which guaranteed full adjudicative procedures in benefits cases, may be dead, the Court’s Mathews v. Eldridge balance-of-interests test for determining what due process is required in an agency case is very much alive. The balance, of course, would have to consider immigration judges, who, as Pierce points out, have no removal protections. But the due process protections accorded immigrants differ from those accorded Social Security beneficiaries, and, importantly, those beneficiaries will have had their protection of independence under the APA taken away if we “give up on agency adjudication.”

Moreover, an attack on ALJ removal protections would presumably apply to all Article I judges—judges who, like ALJs, enjoy statutory protections against removal and do not reside in the judicial branch—including those who make up the U.S. Tax Court and similar entities. Who would want to appear before such bodies if their judges are at-will employees? And what about magistrate judges, whose positions are also established under Article I? Would the federal judiciary really want them to lose removal protections? Doubtful. Now, perhaps the Court could limit these possibilities by reclassifying ALJs as “employees,” not inferior officers, so as to preserve the independence of other Article I judges. After all, it carved out a flimsy exception to save Federal Reserve Board independence in the Trump v. Cook case. But that only presents another problem that looms on the horizon: removal protections for the civil service.

The Trump Administration has already carved out an exception from civil service tenure protections for policymaking employees under its Schedule Policy/Career—and cases challenging that schedule’s exception are pending. There is some possibility that the entire civil service could be at risk of losing its protections from summary, at-will removal. At present, once government employees pass a probationary period, they are entitled to notice and a hearing before being removed. This hearing is supposed to take place before the Merit Systems Protection Board, whose commissioners have already lost their own independence. Still, allowing the protection from removal of ALJs by making them “employees” raises a multitude of problems that the Court must already see or at least sense.

Pierce mentions Jarkesy v. Securities and Exchange Commission and the fact that the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit ruled that ALJ removal restrictions are unconstitutional, in addition to ruling that the Seventh Amendment’s jury-trial right requires securities fraud claims for civil penalties to be adjudicated in federal court rather than before the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). On appeal, the Supreme Court upheld the Fifth Circuit’s Seventh Amendment ruling—by narrowing the so-called public-rights exception that allows agencies rather than federal courts to decide many cases—but declined to address the Fifth Circuit’s ruling about ALJ removal restrictions. I do not think that the unaddressed part of the Fifth Circuit decision about ALJ removal restrictions portends the future. By narrowing the public rights doctrine under the Seventh Amendment, the Jarkesy Court got many cases important to the business community out of the administrative adjudication system. This may be a good place for the Court to stop. Going further, by denying ALJ’s removal protection and repudiating the APA itself, would cast a shadow on all Article I judges and vastly increase the docket of the federal courts.

Admittedly, I am declaring a pyrrhic victory from the fact that the Jarkesy Court did not speak to the Fifth Circuit’s ruling on ALJ removal restrictions. The APA’s formal adjudication provisions were meant to apply to regulatory proceedings, such as those before the SEC and other agencies previously deemed independent. And Jarkesy’s Seventh Amendment reasoning has the potential to vastly limit the scope of those proceedings.

Moreover, the ALJs at issue in Jarkesy are certainly not the dominant decisionmakers in the administrative state. They are vastly outnumbered by administrative judges, in immigration, veterans benefits, and other mass justice settings. These deciders already serve without the protections of the APA’s formal adjudication provisions, including protections from “for cause” removal. Still, holding the line on ALJ independence respects the concept of formal adjudication and preserves the possibility of later including removal protections for all agency adjudicators as part of a basic set of procedural rights under the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause.

Paul R. Verkuil is a former Chair and current senior fellow of the Administrative Conference of the United States.
